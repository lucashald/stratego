# Combat, explained for a new player

## Melee

When two formations meet, both sides roll for it.

Everyone starts with **one d6**. You get to roll an **extra die** (and keep
whichever comes up best) for each of these that's true for you:

- You're the **heavier** formation of the two - Light, Medium, or Heavy,
  compared against your opponent. Two Heavies fighting each other get
  nothing from this; it's about who's bigger *right now*, not an absolute
  bonus.
- You're the **stronger** formation of the two, by current Strength. Same
  idea - only matters if you actually outmatch what's in front of you.
- You're **Cavalry, and you're charging** (you moved into this fight).
- You're **Infantry, and you're defending** (something charged into you).

So a fresh Heavy Infantry sitting still, defending against a charging Light
Cavalry, is rolling three dice (heavier, defending) against the Cavalry's
one or two (charging, plus stronger if it happens to be). Whoever has more
dice isn't guaranteed to win - it's still a die roll - but they're much more
likely to roll well.

Take your best single die and add your current **Strength**. Whoever's
total is higher wins the clash. A **6** on any of your dice is a critical
hit, worth +1 damage on top of everything else - and if you rolled more
than one 6, each one counts.

**Damage:** the losing side gets hurt. How much is whichever is bigger out
of two things - how far apart the two totals ended up, or the winner's own
die roll by itself - minus the loser's **Armor**, plus any crit bonus. A
result of 0 or less just means no damage, not a wasted turn.

**A tie** doesn't mean nothing happened - both sides land a glancing blow,
based on the other side's roll, reduced by their own Armor. Standing your
ground and rolling well is never a completely free pass for the other side.

The upshot: knowing an enemy's Strength actually tells you something now.
A formation you know is weak is a formation you can commit to fighting with
real confidence, not just a coin flip with a small nudge attached.

## Ranged (Archers)

An archer doesn't need the target to be adjacent, and the target doesn't
get to fight back the way it would in melee - but it does get to try to
avoid the shot.

**The archer** rolls **two d6s and keeps the higher one**, plus their
Strength. That's their shot.

**The target** rolls a number of d6s equal to its own **Armor** - a Light
target (0 Armor) doesn't roll at all, its total is just its bare Strength;
a Medium target rolls 1 die, a Heavy target rolls 2 and keeps the best. Add
the target's Strength to whatever it rolled (or nothing, if it's Light).

If the archer's total is higher, it's a **hit**. Damage is the **margin** -
how much the archer's total beat the target's by - minus the target's
Armor, plus any crit bonus.

A **6** on the archer's dice is a crit, same as melee - but a heavily
armored target gets a chance to shrug it off: if the target also rolls a 6
among its own defense dice, it cancels one of the archer's crits. Roll
two 6s as the archer and the target only rolls one back, and one crit
still gets through.

Here's the part that matters even when the shot doesn't land: **an
uncancelled crit still does a point of damage even on a miss.** A weak
archer shooting at something far too tough to actually threaten will
almost never win the roll outright - but a lucky 6, if the target doesn't
roll one back, still finds a gap in the armor. Even a hopeless shot isn't
completely wasted.

## Why both work the same way

Both systems share the same shape on purpose: build a pool of dice from
your formation's traits, keep the best one, add Strength, and a 6 is always
a crit worth +1. Melee compares two rolled totals head-to-head; ranged
compares an archer's roll against a stationary target's bare toughness.
Learn one, and the other isn't a new set of rules - it's the same idea
pointed in a different direction.
