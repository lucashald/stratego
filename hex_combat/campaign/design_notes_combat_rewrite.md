# Combat, explained for a new player

## Melee

When two formations meet, both sides roll for it.

Everyone starts with **one d6**. You get an **extra die** for each of these
that's true for you:

- You're the **heavier** formation of the two. Two Heavies fighting each other
  get nothing from this; it's about who's bigger than who's in front of them,
  not an absolute bonus.
- You're the **stronger** formation of the two, by current Strength. Same idea:
  it only matters if you actually outmatch what you're facing.
- You're **Cavalry, and you're charging** (you moved into this fight).
- You're **Infantry, and you're defending** (something charged into you).

So a fresh Heavy Infantry standing its ground against a charging Light Cavalry
rolls three dice (heavier, defending) against the Cavalry's one or two
(charging, plus stronger if it happens to be). More dice doesn't guarantee a
win. It's still a roll. But it makes a good result much more likely, and the
extra dice never raise your ceiling: 6 is 6 whether you rolled one die or four.

Keep your **single best die** and add your current **Strength**. Higher total
wins the clash and takes the square.

Damage comes from two separate places, and it's worth keeping them apart in
your head.

**The gap between the two totals.** Beat them by three, they lose three
Strength. Only the loser pays this, and a tie means nobody pays it.

**Your surviving 6s.** Every **6** you roll is one extra damage. But 6s
**cancel across the two sides, one for one.** If you both roll a single 6,
neither of you gets anything for it. Two 6s against their one leaves you one
that still counts. Your own two 6s don't cancel each other; only theirs cancel
yours.

The important part: **6s don't care who won.** They land on a win, on a draw,
and on a loss. So:

- A formation being ridden down can still put a 6 through whatever is killing
  it. You go down, but you take something with you.
- A draw isn't always free. Nobody pays the gap, but if one side rolled a 6 the
  other couldn't match, that still costs a point.
- Because 6s cancel, only one side can ever be owed crit damage in a single
  fight. It's never a mutual bloodbath out of nowhere.

**You still cannot destroy a healthy formation in one fight.** The gap is
rarely more than five or six, and a crit adds a point or two at most. Killing
something takes repeated fights, or catching it already hurt.

**There's no Armor.** Being the heavier formation gets you a die instead. That
die *is* your armor.

The upshot: knowing an enemy's Strength now tells you two separate things. It
tells you how hard they hit, and it tells you whether you're getting the
Strength die when you fight them. Wearing a formation down doesn't just lower
its damage. Once it drops below yours, it loses a die too, and the fight tilts
faster than the Strength numbers alone suggest.

## Ranged (Archers)

An archer doesn't need the target adjacent, and the target never shoots back.
But it does get to try to ride out the shot, and it can simply outroll you.

Ranged attacks are chosen during the universal post-clash action phase. An
Archer chooses either one adjacent reposition step or one ranged attack. All
reposition movement and its battles resolve first; surviving Archers then fire
from their final hexes at their targets' final hexes. Cavalry can therefore
defeat an Archer before it shoots, while a target at range 2 can escape to
range 3. Aimed fire follows a selected formation, and Volley attacks whoever
finishes on a selected hex.

**The archer** rolls the base die, plus the **heavier** and **stronger** dice
if they apply, plus **one more when firing at range 1**. A range-2 shot gets no
extra die. Keep the best one, add Strength.

**The target** rolls the base die plus its own **heavier** and **stronger**
dice. It gets no role die and no range die. Keep the best, add Strength.

If the archer's total is **strictly higher**, the shot lands for the **margin**.
On top of that, any 6s that survived cancelling do their damage regardless of
who won the contest, exactly as in melee.

That last part matters most when the shot had no business landing. An archer
firing at something far too tough will lose the contest almost every time, but
a 6 the target doesn't match still finds a gap. You are never firing for
literally nothing. The target's own 6s are its only defence against that. A
shot that lost the contest and still drew blood is reported as a **graze**
rather than a hit.

That makes range a real decision rather than a special cost. A range-1 shot is
more accurate. A range-2 shot buys you standoff distance and nothing else.

## Why both work the same way

Both share one shape on purpose: build a pool of dice from what's true about
this particular matchup, keep the best one, add Strength, take the gap as
damage if you lost, and add surviving 6s whatever happened. Melee compares two
rolled totals head-to-head. Ranged does the same thing, minus the role die and
plus the short shot. Learn one and you've learned the other pointed in a
different direction.

## What was deliberately left out

This is a stripped baseline, not the finished system. Cover, high ground for
archers, and an armor stat were all considered and left out on purpose, so that
each one can be added back on its own and measured against this. If a later
rule makes fights better, we want to know it was that rule.
